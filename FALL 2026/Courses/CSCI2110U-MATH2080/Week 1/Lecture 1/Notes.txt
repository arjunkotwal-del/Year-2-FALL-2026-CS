----------------------------------------------------
1. WHAT IS A PROPOSITION?
----------------------------------------------------

Definition: A declarative sentence that is either
true or false - not both, not neither.

Propositions:      "Toronto is in Canada" (T)
                    "2 + 2 = 5" (F)

NOT propositions:   "What time is it?" (a question)
                    "x + 1 = 2" (depends on x, not fixed)

We use lowercase letters to represent them: p, q, r, s...
True/False written as T/F or 1/0.

----------------------------------------------------
2. THE SIX CONNECTIVES
----------------------------------------------------

Name            Symbol      Read as
----            ------      -------
Negation        NOT p       "not p"
Conjunction     p AND q     "p and q"
Disjunction     p OR q      "p or q" (inclusive - or both)
Exclusive or    p XOR q     "p xor q" (exactly one, not both)
Conditional     p -> q      "if p then q"
Biconditional   p <-> q     "p if and only if q"

Example set-up used throughout:
  p = "I just won the lottery"
  q = "I am buying everyone a pony"

  NOT p        = "I did not just win the lottery."
  p AND q      = "I just won the lottery and I am buying everyone a pony."
  p OR q       = "I just won the lottery or I am buying everyone a pony" (or both).
  p XOR q      = "Exactly one of those two things is true - not both."

----------------------------------------------------
3. TRUTH TABLES FOR THE CONNECTIVES
----------------------------------------------------

NEGATION:
  p | NOT p
  T |  F
  F |  T

AND / OR / XOR:
  p | q | p AND q | p OR q | p XOR q
  T | T |    T     |   T    |    F
  T | F |    F     |   T    |    T
  F | T |    F     |   T    |    T
  F | F |    F     |   F    |    F

CONDITIONAL (p -> q)  <-- the one everyone gets wrong:
  p | q | p -> q
  T | T |   T
  T | F |   F   <-- ONLY false case
  F | T |   T
  F | F |   T

WHY is False -> anything TRUE?
A conditional is a PROMISE: "if p, then q."
The promise is only BROKEN when p happens and q doesn't.
If p never happens (p is False), the promise was never
tested, so it's vacuously true.
Example: "If Juan has a smartphone, then 2+3=5" is TRUE
if Juan has no smartphone - no matter how false "2+3=5" is.

BICONDITIONAL (p <-> q) - true exactly when p and q MATCH:
  p | q | p <-> q
  T | T |   T
  T | F |   F
  F | T |   F
  F | F |   T

----------------------------------------------------
4. CONVERSE, INVERSE, CONTRAPOSITIVE
----------------------------------------------------

Given: p -> q

  Converse:        q -> p           (swap p and q)
  Inverse:         NOT p -> NOT q   (negate both, keep order)
  Contrapositive:  NOT q -> NOT p   (swap AND negate)

*** KEY FACT TO MEMORIZE ***
p -> q and its CONTRAPOSITIVE (NOT q -> NOT p) are
LOGICALLY EQUIVALENT - always the same truth value.

The converse and inverse are equivalent to EACH OTHER,
but NOT to the original statement.
Assuming converse = original is one of the most common
logic errors, and it matters a lot later for proofs
(contrapositive proofs show up in Sections 1.7-1.8).

WORKED EXAMPLE:
"The home team wins whenever it is raining."

Rewrite first: "If it is raining, then the home team wins."
  p = it is raining
  q = the home team wins
  So the statement is: p -> q

  Contrapositive (NOT q -> NOT p):
    "If the home team does not win, then it is not raining."
    -> EQUIVALENT to original

  Converse (q -> p):
    "If the home team wins, then it is raining."
    -> NOT equivalent

  Inverse (NOT p -> NOT q):
    "If it is not raining, then the home team does not win."
    -> NOT equivalent

----------------------------------------------------
5. TRANSLATING ENGLISH INTO LOGIC
----------------------------------------------------

Key phrases that all mean p -> q:
  "if p, then q"
  "q whenever p"
  "q if p"
  "p is sufficient for q"
  "q is necessary for p"
  "p only if q"

*** WATCH OUT: "ONLY IF" IS A TRAP ***
"p only if q" translates to  p -> q,  NOT  q -> p.

Say it slowly: "You can graduate only if you pass the exam"
means passing is REQUIRED (necessary) for graduating -
graduating (p) forces passing (q) to be true.
So: p -> q.

WORKED EXAMPLE:
"You cannot ride the roller coaster if you are under 4 feet
tall unless you are older than 16 years old."

  q = "You can ride the roller coaster"
  r = "You are under 4 feet tall"
  s = "You are older than 16"

  "Unless" behaves like "if not" - so "you cannot ride
  UNLESS s" means: if NOT s (and you're under 4 feet),
  then you cannot ride.

  Formula:  (r AND NOT s) -> NOT q

6. TRUTH TABLES OF COMPOUND PROPOSITIONS
----------------------------------------------------

Bookkeeping rule:
  n variables -> 2^n rows.
  List variables in alphabetical order.
  Column for the i-th variable (counting from 1) alternates
  in blocks of 2^(n-i) T's then F's.

Example: 3 variables p, q, r -> 8 rows
  p column: T T T T F F F F
  q column: T T F F T T F F
  r column: T F T F T F T F

WORKED EXAMPLE - build truth table for:
  (p OR NOT q) -> (p AND q)

  p | q | NOT q | p OR NOT q | p AND q | RESULT
  T | T |   F   |     T      |    T    |   T
  T | F |   T   |     T      |    F    |   F
  F | T |   F   |     F      |    F    |   T
  F | F |   T   |     T      |    F    |   F

Build it column by column, left to right. Never try to
jump straight to the final column in your head.

7. OPERATOR PRECEDENCE (BRACKETS)
----------------------------------------------------

When parentheses are dropped, apply operators in THIS order
(highest priority = evaluated/grouped FIRST):

  Priority 1 (first):  NOT   (¬)
  Priority 2:          AND   (∧)
  Priority 3:          OR    (∨)
  Priority 4:          IF-THEN (→)
  Priority 5 (last):   IFF   (↔)

Think of it like PEMDAS in arithmetic - the order is just
memorized, not something you can "feel out" intuitively.

Rule of thumb: NOT grabs only the single symbol right next
to it. AND clumps its immediate neighbors next. Then OR.
Then the conditional. Whatever is LOWEST priority ends up
as the OUTERMOST operator of the whole formula.

WORKED EXAMPLE:
  Formula:  NOT p AND q OR r -> s

  Step 1 - find all NOTs first.
    Only one, right before p. It only touches p, nothing else.
    -> (NOT p) AND q OR r -> s

  Step 2 - find AND.
    One AND, between (NOT p) and q. Group those two.
    -> ((NOT p) AND q) OR r -> s

  Step 3 - find OR.
    One OR, between that block and r. Group those two.
    -> (((NOT p) AND q) OR r) -> s

  Step 4 - find ->.
    One ->, between that whole block and s. This is LAST
    (lowest priority), so it becomes the outer shell.

  FINAL FULLY-PARENTHESIZED FORM:
    ((NOT p AND q) OR r) -> s

A TRICKIER CASE (the one that actually catches people):

  p -> q OR r

Common mistake: people think "-> comes first because it's
the main point of the sentence." WRONG.
Check the table: OR (priority 3) beats -> (priority 4).
So OR groups FIRST:

  p -> (q OR r)         <-- correct
  NOT (p -> q) OR r     <-- wrong grouping

These are genuinely different formulas (they can produce
different truth values for the same p, q, r), so don't
assume they match.

EXAM HABIT: Even though precedence rules exist, USE
PARENTHESES ANYWAY when writing your own formulas. It
removes all ambiguity and costs you nothing.

----------------------------------------------------
8. BIT STRINGS AND BIT OPERATIONS
----------------------------------------------------

A bit string is a sequence of 0s/1s (1 = true, 0 = false).
Apply connectives bit-by-bit (strings must be equal length):
  OR, AND, XOR

WORKED EXAMPLE:
  x = 0110 1011
  y = 1110 0001

    0110 1011
    1110 0001
    ---------
    1110 1011   OR   (1 if EITHER bit is 1)
    0110 0001   AND  (1 only if BOTH bits are 1)
    1000 1010   XOR  (1 only if bits DIFFER)
