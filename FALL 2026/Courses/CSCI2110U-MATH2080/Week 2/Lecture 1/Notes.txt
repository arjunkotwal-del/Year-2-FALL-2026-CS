----------------------------------------------------
1. TAUTOLOGY, CONTRADICTION, CONTINGENCY
----------------------------------------------------

TAUTOLOGY:    always True, no matter the inputs.
              Example: p OR NOT p

CONTRADICTION: always False, no matter the inputs.
               Example: p AND NOT p

CONTINGENCY:  neither - sometimes T, sometimes F.

Reminder shortcut: spotting "(anything) OR T" or
"X OR NOT X" lets you call it a tautology instantly -
but you should still be able to build the table to
confirm it if unsure.

----------------------------------------------------
2. LOGICAL EQUIVALENCE - FORMAL DEFINITION
----------------------------------------------------

DEFINITION: p and q are LOGICALLY EQUIVALENT (written
p = q, or p <=> q) if p <-> q is a tautology -
equivalently, if their truth tables have IDENTICAL
columns for every row.

IMPORTANT: "=" here (logical equivalence) is NOT a
connective. "p = q" is not itself a compound proposition -
it's a STATEMENT ABOUT two propositions, meaning
"these always match."

HOW TO CHECK: build both truth tables side by side.
If the final columns match row-for-row, they're equivalent.

----------------------------------------------------
3. DE MORGAN'S LAWS (the headline result of this section)
----------------------------------------------------

  NOT (p AND q)  =  (NOT p) OR (NOT q)
  NOT (p OR q)   =  (NOT p) AND (NOT q)

MEMORY RULE: negating flips AND<->OR and pushes the
negation onto each piece individually.

WORKED EXAMPLE (proved via truth table):
Show NOT(p OR q) = (NOT p) AND (NOT q)

  p | q | p OR q | NOT(p OR q) | NOT p | NOT q | NOT p AND NOT q
  T | T |   T    |      F      |   F   |   F   |        F
  T | F |   T    |      F      |   F   |   T   |        F
  F | T |   T    |      F      |   T   |   F   |        F
  F | F |   F    |      T      |   T   |   T   |        T

Columns 4 and 7 match exactly -> equivalent. Confirmed.

APPLIED EXAMPLE:
Negate: "Miguel has a cellphone and he has a laptop computer."

  p = "Miguel has a cellphone"
  q = "Miguel has a laptop"
  Statement = p AND q

  Negation, by De Morgan's: NOT(p AND q) = (NOT p) OR (NOT q)

  RESULT: "Miguel does not have a cellphone, or he does
  not have a laptop computer."

*** COMMON MISTAKE TO AVOID ***
The negation of "p and q" is NOT "not p and not q."
It's "not p OR not q." Negating FLIPS the connective -
this is exactly the trap quiz questions test.

----------------------------------------------------
4. THE CONDITIONAL-DISJUNCTION EQUIVALENCE
----------------------------------------------------

  p -> q   =   (NOT p) OR q

This is huge - it lets you rewrite ANY conditional as an
OR, which is often easier to manipulate algebraically (no
separate "implication" rules to memorize once converted).

WORKED CHECK (truth table):

  p | q | NOT p | NOT p OR q | p -> q
  T | T |   F   |     T      |   T
  T | F |   F   |     F      |   F
  F | T |   T   |     T      |   T
  F | F |   T   |     T      |   T

Match -> confirmed equivalent.

----------------------------------------------------
5. THE MASTER TABLE OF EQUIVALENCES
----------------------------------------------------

Identity laws:      p AND T = p          p OR F = p
Domination laws:     p OR T = T          p AND F = F
Idempotent laws:     p OR p = p          p AND p = p
Double negation:     NOT(NOT p) = p
Commutative laws:    p OR q = q OR p     p AND q = q AND p
Associative laws:    (p OR q) OR r = p OR (q OR r)
                     (same pattern for AND)
Distributive laws:   p OR (q AND r) = (p OR q) AND (p OR r)
                     p AND (q OR r) = (p AND q) OR (p AND r)
De Morgan's laws:    NOT(p AND q) = NOT p OR NOT q
                     NOT(p OR q)  = NOT p AND NOT q
Absorption laws:     p OR (p AND q) = p
                     p AND (p OR q) = p
Negation laws:       p OR NOT p = T      p AND NOT p = F

TIP - you've already used absorption:
Remember when we simplified
  (NOT p OR q) OR (NOT p AND q)
down to just
  NOT p OR q  ?
That was the ABSORPTION LAW in action:
  p OR (p AND q) = p,   with "p" standing in for "NOT p"

DON'T memorize these by rote - recognize the SHAPE.
Most exam questions are "apply one law at a time" chains,
not "recall the name."

----------------------------------------------------
6. BUILDING NEW EQUIVALENCES ALGEBRAICALLY
   (no truth table needed)
----------------------------------------------------

This is the actual skill tested - chain known
equivalences together, one substitution at a time,
citing which law you used at each step. This scales
better than truth tables (2^n rows explodes fast -
20 variables = over a million rows).

WORKED EXAMPLE:
Show NOT(p -> q) and (p AND NOT q) are equivalent,
by algebra, not a table.

  NOT(p -> q)
    = NOT(NOT p OR q)        [conditional-disjunction equivalence]
    = NOT(NOT p) AND NOT q   [De Morgan's law]
    = p AND NOT q            [double negation law]

Each line is justified by exactly ONE named law - that's
the format you should reproduce in your own work.

SECOND WORKED EXAMPLE:
Show (p AND q) -> (p OR q) is a tautology, by reducing
it to T.

  (p AND q) -> (p OR q)
    = NOT(p AND q) OR (p OR q)      [conditional-disjunction]
    = (NOT p OR NOT q) OR (p OR q)  [De Morgan's]
    = (NOT p OR p) OR (NOT q OR q)  [associative/commutative]
    = T OR T                        [negation law, twice]
    = T                             [domination law]

Since it reduces to the constant T, it's a tautology.

----------------------------------------------------
7. SATISFIABILITY
----------------------------------------------------

A proposition is SATISFIABLE if there's AT LEAST ONE
assignment of truth values that makes it True.
It's UNSATISFIABLE if it's False under EVERY assignment
(i.e. it's a contradiction).

  - To prove satisfiable -> just exhibit one assignment
    that works (a "solution").
  - To prove unsatisfiable -> you need to argue EVERY
    assignment fails (harder - usually by reasoning about
    the structure, not brute-force checking each row).

This is used heavily in practice (n-queens, Sudoku
solvers, circuit design) but at our level it's mostly
about the definition and reasoning through small examples.
